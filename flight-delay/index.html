MayD(el)ay

Predicts whether a US domestic flight will leave 15 minutes late or more, using only what is known an hour before departure: the flight schedule, the airport's recent history and the latest weather.

Code and report

The problem

Delays cost US airlines around $10 billion a year, and a missed delay (a stranded passenger, a crew running out of duty hours, a knock on effect down the line) costs far more than a warning for a flight that leaves on time. So we scored models with F2, which counts recall twice as much as precision, and tuned each model's decision threshold for it. About one flight in five is delayed, so a model that never warns is right 80% of the time and useless.

My part

I worked on keeping the data honest and the pipeline running at scale. I audited every column for leakage, sorting out what is known before departure from what is only recorded after it, and I built the train, validation and test split by time. Through the later phases I owned the preprocessing pipeline: imputation, scaling and assembling about a hundred features into model inputs for 31.7 million flights, saved and checkpointed so the rest of the team could train on it without rebuilding it, and I helped with the modelling. I led the final phase, made the visuals for our final presentation and revised the final report.

How it works

  • Flights joined to weather. Bureau of Transportation Statistics records for every domestic flight from 2015 to 2019, joined to NOAA weather from the nearest station over the three hours ending an hour before departure.
  • No peeking at the answer. More than 25 columns that are only recorded after takeoff were dropped, scaling statistics were frozen at the last training date, and 2019 was held back as a blind test year.
  • Eight feature families. Time of day and calendar, holidays, airport congestion, recent route and carrier delay rates, weather, airport traits and interactions between them, about a hundred inputs in all.
  • Six model families. Logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosted trees in Spark, plus neural networks in PySpark and PyTorch and a Transformer for tabular data.

The models and how they differ

Every model was trained on the earlier years with the class imbalance handled by undersampling or class weights, had its decision threshold chosen on validation, and was then scored once on the blind 2019 test year.

  • Logistic regression is the linear baseline. It needs scaled inputs, trains in about 20 minutes and is easy to explain, but it can only add up features, not combine them.
  • Random forest averages many trees grown on random slices of the data. It handles interactions without hand built features, needs no scaling, and was the fastest to train at about 7 minutes.
  • Gradient boosted trees build trees one after another, each fixing the last one's mistakes. They separated delayed from on time flights best (highest AUROC), but took about 7.5 hours to train with grid search.
  • Neural networks in PySpark train across the cluster but are limited: sigmoid activations only, no dropout and one optimizer. The smallest network, one hidden layer of 64 units, beat the deeper ones, and at the default threshold it caught only 57% of delays.
  • Neural networks in PyTorch gave full control (ReLU, dropout, Adam, batch normalization, early stopping) for architecture and dropout sweeps.
  • A Transformer for tabular data turns each feature into a token and lets attention learn how features interact. It reached a test score competitive with the best trees, without beating them.

What we learned

  • Threshold tuning mattered more than the model. At the default threshold of 0.5 the models miss about two thirds of delays. Once thresholds were tuned, logistic regression, random forest and boosted trees all landed within 0.005 of each other on the test year.
  • Trees beat neural networks on tabular data. The neural networks reached the same range only after tuning, at a much higher training cost.
  • One simple flag says a lot. When the plane's previous flight left late, the delay rate rises from 17% to 28%.
  • There is a ceiling. Mechanical problems, crew shortages and air traffic control delays don't show up in schedules or weather, so catching most delays means about four false alarms for every real one.
