Linear A

Linear A is a 3,500 year old writing system from ancient Crete that nobody can read. Computer studies keep claiming to know which language family it belongs to, and they keep disagreeing. I showed why, and that for now the honest answer is: we can't tell.

The puzzle

Linear B, a later script that uses many of the same symbols, was cracked in 1952 and turned out to be an early form of Greek. Using its sounds, we can read Linear A out loud, but the words don't match any known language. Computer studies have linked it to Semitic languages, to Anatolian languages, and in one paper to five unrelated families at once. When the answers are that scattered, the problem is the method, not just the data.

Three ways a tiny amount of text can fool you

  • Different writing systems. The same word can look unrelated when it's written two ways: Greek khrusós (gold) is ku ru so in Linear B. So I first rewrite every language in the same simple syllable spelling.
  • Different amounts of text. A model trained on a lot of text judges strictly and one trained on a little judges loosely, so their scores can't be compared directly. I used scores that adjust for size, with error bars.
  • Borrowed sounds. Reading Linear A with Linear B's sounds might make it look more Greek than it really is. I measured that effect for the first time by shuffling the sounds randomly 300 times.

What I found

  • The method passes a test with a known answer. It correctly sees that Linear B and the Greek alphabet write the same language.
  • Linear A isn't Greek. Its symbols look like Linear B's, but its words don't look like Greek. A real Greek dialect would match on both.
  • The borrowed sound effect is real. The real sounds make Linear A look more Greek than about 98% of random shuffles, but the main result still holds.
  • A false answer, explained. Without these safeguards, comparing Linear A with ten languages gives a confident “90% Semitic” result, and three checks show it's a side effect of the method.
  • Five different methods agree. Comparing word shapes, weighing the odds with a “none of these” option, looking at borrowed trade words, comparing related scripts and tracing where the symbols came from all give the same answer: we can't tell.

The takeaway

I'm not decoding Linear A. What I offer is a checklist for anyone who tries, on this or any other unread script: test the method on a case where you know the answer, write every language the same way, use fair scores with error bars, and measure the borrowed sound effect. With those in place, a confident answer has to earn its confidence.
